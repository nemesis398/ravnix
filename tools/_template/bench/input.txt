Huginn and Muninn are the two ravens of the old northern stories. Each
morning they leave the high seat and fly over the whole world, and each
evening they come back and tell what they have seen. Huginn is thought and
Muninn is memory, and the one who sends them out fears more for memory than
for thought, because a thought can be had again and a memory, once lost, is
gone.

The stories do not say how far the ravens fly in a day, or what they eat,
or whether they rest on the way. They say that the ravens come back. That is
the whole point of them: two birds that go out into a world that does not
care about them, and return with something worth knowing.

A raven is a patient bird. It watches before it lands, it remembers a face
for years, and it hides food in more places than it will ever need. It
plays in the wind above a cliff for no reason anyone has found. It talks,
in a voice of clicks and croaks and knocks, and it learns the sounds of
other animals and of people. A pair stays together for life and holds its
ground against eagles.

In winter a raven can live where little else does. It follows wolves and
hunters and takes what they leave. It finds the dead elk under the snow and
calls the others. When the light comes back, the pair builds its nest again
on the same ledge, of sticks and bones and wool, and lines it with fur.

The two ravens of the stories are not two halves of one bird. Thought goes
ahead and guesses; memory comes after and keeps. Thought without memory
would make the same mistake every morning, and memory without thought would
only ever tell the same story. The one who sends them out needs both, and
listens to both, and is glad each evening when both come back.

A machine that is meant to help a person think and remember can learn from
the birds. It should go where the work is and come back when it is called.
It should keep what it has seen and not lose it when the lid is closed or
the line drops. It should tell what it knows plainly, in the same words
each time, so that the person who listens can trust it. And it should not
be loud about any of this.

Count the words of a text and you learn little about it. Count how many of
them are distinct and you learn a little more: a short text repeats few
words, and a long one repeats many, the small words most of all. The, and,
of, a, to, in: these come back again and again, like the ravens, every
evening, from every page.
